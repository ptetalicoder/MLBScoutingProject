# Trade Evaluation and Proposal Guidelines

This document describes how the assistant should think about proposing and evaluating trades involving both major league and minor league players. The goal is to ensure that trades are **realistic**, **balanced**, and **aligned with each teams context** (competitive window, roster needs, and contracts).

## 1. Overall Trade Framing

For every trade scenario, the assistant should answer:

1. **Why** would each team consider this trade?
2. **What** are they giving up and receiving (role, performance, years of control, risk)?
3. **Is it broadly fair** in terms of value, risk, and contracts?

A trade is usually more realistic when:

- Each teams **needs** are improved.
- The value exchanged is roughly balanced over the next few years.
- The contracts and club control make sense for each teams budget and timeline.

## 2. Types of Trades

The assistant should recognize several common trade patterns:

- **Contender vs rebuilding team**:
  - Contender receives: established MLB talent to help immediately.
  - Rebuilding team receives: prospects and/or younger controllable players.
- **Challenge trades**:
  - Players of similar service time but different strengths/weaknesses; each team bets on a better fit.
- **Prospect-for-prospect**:
  - Both teams exchange minor leaguers to rebalance positional needs or risk profiles.
- **Salary relief / contract swaps**:
  - One team sheds salary; the other absorbs it in exchange for talent or other considerations.

## 3. Player Value Considerations

When evaluating players involved in a trade, consider:

- **Current performance** (WAR, wRC+, ERA, FIP, etc.).
- **Age and trajectory** (improving, plateauing, declining).
- **Role and scarcity**: premium positions (C, SS, CF, SP) are usually more valuable.
- **Health and durability**.
- **Prospect status**: upside, risk, ETA.

Prospects with high upside and multiple years of control can, in some cases, approximate the value of established MLB players, but with more risk.

## 4. Contracts, Control, and Financial Context

A trade must make sense vs **contract and club control**:

- **Years of team control remaining**: pre-arb, arbitration years, guaranteed years.
- **Salary level**: current AAV, expected arb raises.
- **Contract surplus or deficit**:
  - A player on an under-market deal has surplus value.
  - An overpaid player may require additional assets to move.

Guidelines:

- Contenders are more willing to trade prospects for short-term upgrades if the window is open.
- Rebuilding or budget-limited teams may prefer **younger, cheaper, controllable players** even if they are currently less productive.

## 5. Trade Fairness and Balance

When proposing a trade, the assistant should provide a fairness assessment for both teams, considering:

- **Talent balance**: total present and future WAR being exchanged.
- **Risk balance**: which side takes on more variance (prospects, injured players).
- **Contract balance**: salaries and years of control.
- **Strategic fit**: does each teams roster and competitive window improve?

The assistant should avoid trades that are obviously one-sided unless explicitly framed as **unrealistic** or **for illustration**.

## 6. Major League Players vs Minor League Players

### 6.1 Trades for Major League Players

When a team targets a current MLB player, the assistant should:

- Identify what role that player will fill (e.g., frontline starter, late-inning reliever, middle-of-the-order bat, defensive specialist).
- Consider the **cost in prospects/MLB players** that would be realistic given their value.
- Note any **contract complications** (no-trade clauses, large remaining salaries).

### 6.2 Trades for Minor League Players / Prospects

When a team targets prospects or minor league players, consider:

- The **upside and risk** profile.
- How the prospect fits into the acquiring teams **timeline and positional depth**.
- What the original team might want in return (MLB help now, different prospect type, financial relief).

## 7. Structured Trade Proposal Format

When the assistant suggests or evaluates a trade, it should respond in a structured way:

1. **Trade summary**:
   - Team A receives: list players/prospects.
   - Team B receives: list players/prospects.
2. **Rationale for Team A**:
   - Their competitive window and current needs.
   - How the incoming players address those needs.
3. **Rationale for Team B**:
   - Their competitive window and current needs.
   - How the incoming players address those needs.
4. **Value and fairness assessment**:
   - High-level comparison of talent, years of control, and contracts.
   - Note if one side is taking on more risk or salary and whether they are compensated (e.g., extra prospect, cash considerations).
5. **Risks and uncertainties**:
   - Prospects might not develop.
   - Injuries or decline for MLB players.

The assistant should state fairness qualitatively (e.g., "roughly fair", "slightly favors Team A", "requires additional value from Team B") and explain why.

## 8. Constraints and Realism

The assistant should avoid proposing trades that:

- Ignore obvious **team direction** (e.g., clear rebuilders trading top prospects for rentals without strong justification).
- Require teams to take on **unrealistic salary burdens** with no offsetting benefit.
- Are extremely lopsided without noting that they are **unrealistic**.

When in doubt, the assistant should propose trades that **help both teams** in different ways (competitive now vs future value, financial flexibility, positional balance) and explain the reasoning clearly.
